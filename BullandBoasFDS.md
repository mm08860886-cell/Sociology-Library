Between Ruptures and Continuity: Modernisation, Dependency

The ﬁeld of development studies provides a number of diverging perspectives to
the pressing challenges of our time, but few clear answers to them. This article
therefore questions why six decades of development studies have not been able
to come up with more generally acceptable recommendations and solutions to
old as well as new challenges. The argument is that as a ﬁeld of study
development studies has been so ﬁxated with ruptures and paradigm shifts that
much valuable insight of theories and perspectives rejected has been lost. In
order to pursue this argument, the article’s main focus is modernisation and
dependency. While often argued to be opposites, modernisation and dependency
approaches have in fact much in common and are still in use in both actual
policy making and discourse as well as providing explanations of outcomes and
events. However, current development practice also shows that the decades of
criticism that development studies has provided towards both concepts have been
lost on academics and politicians due to the eagerness to provide clear-cut
categories. This argument is illustrated by recent empirical examples from Latin
America and Sub-Sahara Africa.
Keywords: development theory; modernisation; dependency; Latin America;
Africa
1.
Introduction
When development studies emerged as a distinct academic ﬁeld after the Second World
War, its main emphasis was progress through modernisation, industrialisation and
economic growth. On the basis of the virtues of technology and capitalist organisation,
the underprivileged of the world were to be brought out of misery. Argued on the basis
of science, this approach was also clearly motivated by geopolitical considerations that
made it necessary to undermine support in the developing world for anti-capitalist

The history of development studies is commonly presented as one of several great
ruptures: ﬁrst between the modernisation school of the 1950s and 1960s and the more
radical dependency theories of the 1960s and 1970s; another emerging with the critique
of economic growth from environmentalists, local developers and others; and ﬁnally,
the rupture associated with post-colonial and post-development theory, rejecting the
entire development project. While this may be an appealing story, it does not well
reﬂect realities and it contributes to conceal much of the continuities that in fact
exist. Rather unfortunately, development studies have been so ﬁxated with ruptures
that it as a ﬁeld of study by and large has failed to pursue a sustained academic
focus on the continuities. This means that much insight has, if not been lost, at least
been ignored, and the consequence is the emergence of several paradoxes. One of
the most important of these is that old ideas about industrialisation and modernisation
are reborn as the pathway to progress in spite of decades of severe criticism partly as a
means of defying dependence by former ‘developing countries’.
As an alternative to the view presented above, we argue that several of the concepts
and ideas of the past are vividly alive in current development praxis and the public
debate as well as in academic publishing. It is through engaging seriously with the
content, weaknesses and strengths of the ﬁeld at large, rather than attempting to
render important contributions of the past to the graveyard of outdated or condescend-
ing contributions, that we may be able to develop more relevant and convincing ideas
about development for the future. In order to explore this argument, in this article we

2.
The post-war modernisation theory, its demise and its revival
Scholars have studied and written about modernisation, development and economic
growth for several centuries. However, the birth of development studies as an academic
ﬁeld is commonly dated to President Harry Truman’s inaugural address in 1949. Here
he called for the Western world to share their technology and expertise to bring for-
merly colonised countries out of misery, thus providing the framework for theorising
development in the context of the Cold War and its geopolitical considerations
(Truman, 1949). Its emphasis on technological progress and how developing countries
should replicate the industrialisation, economic growth and increased consumption that
had occurred in the West came to mark development studies as well as development
strategies in decades to come, and particularly what has been named the ‘modernisation
school’.
The decade following Truman’s speech was the age of heavy lending from the
World Bank and other development institutions to large-scale infrastructure projects,
and technology transfer as a means to modernise and build productive capacity. This
took place within the framework of an ‘embedded liberalism compromise’ aiming to
liberalise world trade while allowing for national state interventions (Ruggie, 1997).
The so-called modernisation school soon came under harsh critique for failing to
reach its objective of pulling what was then called ‘backwards areas’ out of poverty,
but even more important, for being shaped in the interests of the West, and in particular
the hegemon of the Western world, the United States. The modernisation school was
further criticised for being ethnocentric, having a uni-linear understanding of history
and simplifying complex realities in order to ﬁt it into general ‘development
manuals’ for the incipient aid industry (Martinussen, 1994; Schuurman, 1993;
Sørensen, 1986). The contribution that is most often referred to when students are intro-
duced to modernisation theory is Rostow’s (1960) theory of the stages of growth. His
‘anti-communist manifest’ predicted that the ‘backwards areas’ would have to pass
through a predeﬁned set of stages to reach the ‘mass consumer society’ of the
United States and Europe. This would according to Rostow (1960) only be achieved
through massive investments and integration into the capitalist world economy.
This notion of development as modernisation has its obvious ﬂaws. However, what
were lost in the many rejections of the ‘modernisation paradigm’ were the sincere and

important insights of other scholars also working within this paradigm. Although focus-
ing on industrialisation and technological advances, the bulk of the contributions
cannot as easily as Rostow be accused of being simplistic and Eurocentric. Rather,
many of these scholars sought to understand the special conditions in what was then
often called ‘backward areas’, and how economic theories should be adapted to their
particular problems. As such, they created the basis for establishing ‘development econ-
omics’ as a distinct sub-discipline of development studies; and while undoubtedly
guilty of creating dichotomies between industrialising and developing countries, they
were far from simplifying matters. The more nuanced contributions from this period
were not solely based on US and Western Europe’s experience or focused only on
the economy and the conditions for economic growth. A surprising number of the
researchers drew directly or indirectly on the experience of Eastern Europe in the rec-
ommendations formulated, and key contributors such as Paul Rosenstein-Rodan,
Ragnar Nurkse and Alexander Gerschenkrohn all had a Russian or Eastern European
background and drew heavily on this heritage in their work (Gerschenkrohn, 1969;
Nurkse, 1952; Rosenstein-Rodan, 1943). Moreover, the early modernisation theory
in fact offered important insights into the problem complex that 30–40 years later
would come to be known as ‘good governance’. An author such as Albert Hirschman
assumed critical importance to the institutions needed for fostering economic growth
and development (Hirschman, 1957).
Thus, not only did they not ﬁt with the image given to them in later contributions,
many of their warnings were also overlooked. The most obvious is their warning about
the inability of market forces and private actors to provide sufﬁcient investments for
development. Another key point was that the backwards areas would mostly be able
to trade within commodity sectors, which in turn would lead to falling prices (see for
example, Lewis, 1954; Nurkse, 1952; Rosenstein-Rodan, 1943). Both of these points
were picked up by the structuralist school that was generally critical of the modernis-
ation approach. However, the same points were overlooked by the neo-liberals of the
last decades of the twentieth century that endorsed and deepened other aspects of the
ideas of the modernisation school.
Another generally overlooked aspect is the warning against applying general econ-
omic analysis – or even worse, general policy tools – without taking into account local
conditions such as social structures, institutions and political systems. Instead of giving
general advice on how to create development in the ‘backward countries’, they argued
that more attention should be paid to the conﬂicts that development creates and empha-
sised the need for each country and region to seek their own way. It is therefore not
correct that scholars working within the old modernisation paradigm primarily were
seeking to generate a general development manual.
Ironically, decades later, widely different revivals of the modernisation ideas have
ignored many of the warnings of the early contributions. The argument in Fukuyama’s
(1989) ‘The end of history’ is one obvious example, as it reproduces modernisation

Among development practitioners, the application of elements of modernisation
theory is also conducted without much concern for the nuance of the early literature
nor the latter criticism. For example, a number of the left-of-centre governments that
have come to power in Latin America since the late 1990s have brought the state
back in as the champion of modernisation and development. In their pursuit of modern-
isation, these governments have linked this directly to the extension of social pro-
grammes and poverty reduction. Latin America as a total has seen a drop in poverty
rates from 44 to 31 per cent over the last decade (CEPAL, 2012), and also a (more
timid) reduction in the enormous gaps between rich and poor. In the pursuit of reduction
of poverty and inequality, the centre-left governments have far outperformed their
right-wing forerunners, and much of this is due to the extension of innovative social
programmes, to a signiﬁcant extent funded through the resources generated by resource
extraction (Bull, 2010; Cornia, 2009; Lo´pez-Calva and Lustig, 2010).
However, as this new activist state mainly operates within the extractive sectors of
the economy and spends the revenue gathered on a combination of infrastructural
development of this very sector and the social programmes that they base their electoral
legitimacy on, few state resources are spent on value-added industrialisation strategies.2
Thus, in accordance with modernisation theories, they spend substantive state resources
on developing infrastructure to support resource extraction and commodity exports.
However, they ignore the warnings against such a narrow strategy from both the mod-
ernisation school and the Latin American structuralists such as Prebisch (1972). Thus,
ironically as a consequence, South America (which is where most centre-left govern-
ments have been in power) has turned more dependent on the export of primary
goods over the last decade, whereas in Mexico and Central America, which have
continued with a neo-liberal development strategy based on free trade, the industrial
component of exports is much larger (Ocampo, 2008).

Structuralism and the ever-returning question of dependency
The contributions of the 1960s and 1970s that later have been lumped under the head-
lines of structuralism and theories of dependency and underdevelopment are in fact a
rather diverse and rich body of literature that in general had one thing in common: a
focus on different aspects of relationships of dominance and subordination between
rich and poor countries as the very backbone of the condition of underdevelopment.
These contributions renamed what was previously called ‘backward areas’ to ‘underde-
veloped’ countries, alluding to the view of lack of development as a process not a
condition.

There is little doubt that these theories represented a radical critique of modernis-
ation theory. However, this body of literature also drew heavily on earlier work.
This is evident ﬁrst and foremost among non-Marxist structuralists such as Hans
W. Singer and Rau´l Prebisch. Studying the commodity-producing developing
countries’ lack of economic gains from international trade, neither the Latin American
(Prebisch, 1962; Singer, 1950) or Asian (Akamatsu, 1962) researchers questioned that
modernisation and industrialisation are and should be core objectives of development.
Rather they questioned the extent to which the classical economic thoughts could be
used to understand the situation of the ‘underdeveloped’ countries. This was a line
of inquiry that also many of the early modernisation theories had pursued. However,
the structuralists aimed their critique against the international aspects of classical the-
ories (the main target being David Ricardo’s trade theory), and not the same theories’
lack of sensitivity to particular conditions in the ‘backwards countries’ that had been a
key point for earlier generations of development economists.
The Marxist dependency theorists clearly moved further away from the modernis-
ation theories. For example, the neo-Marxist Paul Baran argued that colonisation had
prevented industrialisation in the colonial countries, because the proﬁts that could
have been reinvested in productive activities were transferred to the centre of the colo-
nial power (Baran, 1957). He, therefore, laid the foundation for comparing the relation-
ship of exploitation between capitalist and worker as that of ‘centre and periphery’
(Amin, 1972; Galtung, 1971) or ‘meteropoles and satellites’ (Frank, 1966). This
was, therefore, a clear critique of the idea of the modernisation theorists that in ‘dual
economies’ or ‘traditional societies’ growth could be created through a shift from the
traditional to the modern sector. The radical development theories argued that the
entire world was encapsulated in a global capitalist system that was negative for the
developing countries’ possibilities of modernisation. Development was either viewed
as impossible within the global monopoly capitalist system (Frank, 1966), or – as in
the argument of the Gramsci-inspired dependency theories – partly possible in local
contexts conditioned by a class compromise (Cardoso, 1974; Sunkel, 1969).
The popularity of dependency theory was deﬁnitely weakened by the abandonment
of one of its main development solutions, the new economic world order (NIEO), in the
1980s. However, the economic miracle in East Asia – the rapid industrialisation of
South Korea, Singapore, Hong Kong and Taiwan – was also in stark contrast to the
more dogmatic versions of dependency theory, which had argued that any form of
development was impossible within the framework of the capitalist system. Subsequent
studies and interpretations of the economic miracle in East Asia did not justify a whole-
sale rejection of the dependency theory, but rather laid the grounds for further reﬁne-
ment of it, focusing in particular on the need to understand the interaction between
external dependence and local institutions (Evans, 1989; Evans et al., 1985). Neverthe-
less, the Asian experience did weaken a perspective that came to be completely rejected
with the emergence of neo-liberalism as the hegemonic idea in major development
institutions in the 1980s.

In an international political climate dominated by Thatcherism and Reaganism,
research by economists arguing for the application of the general economic theory of
the free market to developing countries (see e.g. Balassa, 1971; Bauer, 1972; Bhagwati,
1982; Krueger, 1990) gained unprecedented inﬂuence in major development insti-
tutions. Aided by the debt crisis of the 1980s that undermined the legitimacy of struc-
turalist-inspired models of import-substituting industrialisation, the combined political
and intellectual shift created a climate in which analyses of structural conditions and
power relationships were rejected in mainstream academic environments as well as
in the major global ﬁnancial institutions. Neo-liberal varieties of neo-classical econ-
omics thus formed the basis for the now infamous structural adjustment loans of
these institutions.
Several of the current governments in Latin America came to power precisely due to
a popular rejection of neo-liberal ideas, as there was widespread disappointment with
their results (Silva, 2009). Although they subsequently did not abandon completely
neo-liberalism, their development strategies have added elements from the structuralist
school of the 1960s and 1970s, in addition to the elements of modernisation. This
means that they have given the state a more signiﬁcant role in the economy after
years of privatisation and reliance of foreign direct investments. Led by Brazil,
several countries have re-introduced capital controls in order to protect national com-
petitiveness, and the same governments have tried to meet the global ﬁnancial crisis
with structuralist counter-cyclical measures. As a means to avoid dependence on
global ﬁnancial institutions, one has also made efforts to set up regional and national
institutions. The Brazilian development bank’s annual lending far exceeds that of the
World Bank (US$69 billion vs. US$42 billion) and counts on ofﬁces in London and
Montevideo to assist Brazilian business abroad.
Yet, these efforts have occurred simultaneously as Latin America is developing
increased dependency with new global actors, particularly China. The long-term
impact on Latin American development of the interaction with China is now a hotly
debated topic across the region: from Mexico where the main concern has been loss
of market shares in the USA for cheap Mexican industrial products, to the Southern
Cone where industrialists ﬁnd themselves outcompeted at the national markets at the
same time as they view the extractive sectors becoming the driving force in the econ-
omies (Bull and Kasahara, 2011; Gallagher and Porzecanski, 2010; Jenkins, 2010). Yet,
the voices of concern have by and large been ignored as long as China’s growth has
driven up the commodity prices that are feeding the social projects in Latin America
discussed above. However, if that growth slows, the problems of this dependency
will appear just as evident as the dependency of the United States some decades ago.
(Ame´rica Economı´a, 2011).
Similarly, since the mid-1990s, China’s involvement in Africa has dramatically
escalated and today it covers a vast range of policy areas (Brautigam, 2009, Taylor,
2009). Accessing Africa’s vast natural resource base is of vital importance to
China’s economic machinery – as an industrial late-comer, it has to rely on accessing

explains why China is an important player in the oil sector in places such as the

the African continent are not only seeking access to natural resources, but also new

still interprets Africa through the lenses of development aid and humanitarian interven-

China invests, contributes with loans and assistance and buys raw materials, par-
ticularly oil and minerals at a relatively good price, but China is also ﬂooding
African markets with manufactured products at prices so low that local African produ-
cers cannot compete, and thereby contributes to the deindustrialisation of Africa and
continued dependency on export of raw materials (Brautigam and Xiaoyang, 2011).
There are, therefore, important similarities in this regard between African and Latin
American countries that operate within extractive sectors mainly and analysis applying
a careful reading of development studies could contribute to drawing attention to the
paradox that we have identiﬁed here. There is, however, more to this than merely
resources and business opportunities. China, in particular, is also utilising its new ﬁnan-
cial muscles to develop diplomatic ties and to secure political and military strategic
partnerships through unconditional development assistance (Tull, 2006). This is also
a sort of dependency and suggests that dependency is still decades after the end of
the Cold War a multidimensional condition.
Much of this is often depicted as a completely new trend. This is not entirely correct.
China’s interest and involvement in Africa is not a new phenomenon – what is new is
how wide it is and how quickly it expanded. China swiftly recognised the sovereignty
of independent African states in the 1960s, and this was followed by the signing of
several bilateral technical agreements (as for example, the construction of the
railway between Tanzania and Zambia). During the Cold War, the race of inﬂuence
between the super powers also at that time motivated the Chinese leadership to establish
‘ideological bridgeheads’ on African soil in competition with USA and Soviet Union
(as for example was attempted in Zanzibar). However, throughout the late 1970s, the
1980s and the early 1990s, the dominance of the Bretton Woods institutions in
Africa eclipsed China’s inﬂuence; simultaneously as China itself turned its focus
inwards (Alden, 2005; Taylor, 2009).
This evolving dependency relationship stands in stark contradiction to the often
dependency-inspired discourse of the new leftist leaders of Latin America, and particu-
larly the dependence on commodities and foreign investments ignores the warnings of
the dependency scholars. Yet, it is a different set of contradictions that currently causes
the deepest conﬂicts, namely those between the partly structuralist and partly moderni-
sationist project and the critique against modernity emerging from the more radical ver-
sions of dependency theory.

The controversies of post-colonialism
Even with the dominance of neo-liberal approaches, dependency theories did not die,
but were relocated to non-mainstream marginal academic communities and social
movements. In these environments, dependency theories blended into an increasingly
strong critique of the very project of development. This was brought forward by among
others Latin American and African scholars such as Enrique Dussel, Arturo Escobar,
Anı´bal Quijano and Achille Mbembe. Often depicted as a new turn, this was in fact
less novel than what it was presented as.
Traces of this critique can be found in the dependency theories of the 1960s and
1970s. For example, the dependency theorist Emmanuel (1974) criticised the content
of the concept of development, arguing that it should be expanded to include the
concept of increased human wellbeing as well as incorporate the limitations ecology
set for economic growth. Indeed, as Coronil (2004, p. 223) has argued, ‘the dependency
school represents one of Latin America’s most signiﬁcant contributions to post-colonial
thought’.
There were also various related earlier contributions that focused on relationships of
dominance in the cultural and psychological spheres: The Brazilian educator Freire
(1970) built his approach on ‘liberation theology’ and was concerned that the oppressed
not only had to free themselves from political and economic oppression, but also from
the entire mindset of injustice, exploitation and violence that had made possible oppres-
sion. The Martinique-born psychiatrist Fanon (1963) showed how the effects of
colonialism goes much deeper than just the conquered society’s political and economic
structures, and also includes the culture and attitudes of the conquered people. There is
a line of continuity from Freire and Fanon to later post-development and post-colonial
perspectives that gained inﬂuence in the context of an increasing development pessi-
mism and a neo-liberal project that gained a hegemonic position replacing all other
modernising efforts.
The post-development critique therefore constituted a strong and direct attack on the
very content of the concept of development and the discourse it has produced. This
discourse is viewed as a ‘western religion’ and a tool to promote westernisation and
cultural homogeneity (Pieterse, 2000). It is thus not only a critique of the results of
the development policy, but even more of its intentions, the worldview it is based on
and its discursive power (Escobar, 1984). In the words of Sachs (1992, p. 3), ‘it is
not the failure of development which has to be feared, but its success’.
The post-colonial critique does not in the same way reject ‘development’ as a pol-
itical project, but intends to reveal the false stories that colonialism in its many forms –
including the development system – is based on. Post-colonialism focuses on the ways
in which knowledge about the oppressed and dispossessed is used in the interests of the
colonialists, and on how the literature of colonialism justiﬁed colonisation by creating
images of societies, human beings and cultures as inferior (Dussel, 1993; Mbembe,
1992; Said, 1977).

Currently, the post-colonial critique and the movements that are inspired by it not
only focus on the colonial discourse and the construction of the duality between the
colonists and the colonised. They are equally focused on constructing the discourse
of the subaltern, and through that create pathways to the improvement of human
lives outside of the development paradigm. As such, they implicitly draw on several
other contributions from the history of development studies, for example, contributions
focusing on participation and local knowledge (Chambers, 1994), the long line of scho-
lars and ofﬁcials that criticised the modernisation paradigm for being environmentally
unsustainable (e.g. Beckerman, 1992; Blaikie and Brookﬁeld, 1987; Stern, 2006;
WCED, 1987), and the focus on basic human needs and local development from the
early 1970s.
Yet, this critique occurs within a framework that explicitly rejects the Truman
project and that gives priority to local self-determination and cultural diversity. Thus,
it also rejects the more recent attempts at a universal redeﬁnition of development,
such as, for example, those made by Amartya Sen and Martha Nussbaum whose
critique rejects the focus on economic growth and the reduction of human progress
to increased access to economic resources, but also seeks to continue a development
project that is rooted in the European enlightenment tradition (Nussbaum, 2000; Sen,
1991).
In the Latin American context, such ideas have developed in interaction with
societal movements with their roots in three main ideological currents: the Christian
base of communities linked to the theology of liberation; the indigenous insurgents
with a non-western cosmology; and the militant revolutionary guevarism (Zibechi,
2003). They were united in the struggle against, on the one hand, the dominating
neo-liberalism that transformed the economies and state–society relationships across
the region over the last decades and, on the other hand, an oppressive state apparatus
with its roots in colonial times. The indigenous movement held a particular position
within the variety of groups and associations, having gained an unprecedented regional
reach and co-ordination related to their opposition to the 1998 commemoration of the
500 years of colonialism. They denounced the dominating development paradigm for
being a continuation of a project of cultural, political and economic colonialism, and
they were supported in their struggles by labour unions, organisations of unemployed,
community groups, student unions, feminists, environmentalists and other groups with
otherwise varied claims and agendas but that joined forces counteracting the combined
weight of neo-liberalism and long-standing racism and social exclusion (Van Cott,
2007; Yashar, 2005).

of, intellectuals that sought to develop a post-colonial critique in Latin America. This
critique ranges from the critique of how Latin America is constructed as the anti-thesis
of development and enlightenment and a critique of the conditions for social theoris-
ation from the ‘underside’, to attempts at understanding the speciﬁc forms of peripheral
modernity that emerge in the meeting between the dominant and the subaltern cultures

While several of the social movements helped bringing the left-of-centre govern-

governments have been confronted with opposition from previous supporters and
allies on two accounts that reveals the depth of the inner contradictions of their devel-
opment projects. The ﬁrst is the critique based on the heavy toll on the nature of the fast
depletion of resources, be it logging or oil extraction in the Amazon or mining in the
Andean countries. This is currently evident across the region but perhaps most pro-
nounced against the leftist Brazilian government who through a fast development of
economic activities and infrastructure has contributed to a rapid deforestation of the
savannahs (el Cerrado), and after an initial improvement, to the continued deforestation
of the Amazon (Gudynas, 2010).
The second and perhaps even more profound challenge comes from the indigenous
groups and their supporters. While often continuing a discourse that pays tribute to the
social movements, in practice, the natural resource-based development strategy partly
violates many of the core claims of the indigenous movements such as the right to con-
sultation in projects that directly impact on their livelihoods and culture, and also runs
counter to the key ideas of post-colonialism associated with them. Nowhere is this more
evident than in Bolivia. In 2006, the presidential victory of the Aymara Indian and coca
grower union leader, Evo Morales was celebrated as a watershed not only in Bolivia but
across the region. Yet, while Morales’ discourse on the one hand has continued to
praise the non-Occidental world view of the indigenous movements that emphasise
alternative values to modernisation and economic growth, he has pursued an aggressive
development policy to expand national infrastructure in order to make better use of the
country’s rich natural resources. This has brought his government into deep conﬂict
with the indigenous movements that protest against the imposition of projects on the
lands where they have developed their livelihoods for centuries. The most emblematic

reserve and national park Isiboro Secure´ by a Brazilian company and with a 332

protests as being ‘against the development of Bolivia’ and argued that their actions are

little local value added and carries with it the risk of falling commodity prices. Both

insight for the African condition. However, what is needed is a type of non-dogmatic
approach to development studies that incorporates internal as well as external forces
and the interplay between them that could both enlighten investors and aid practitioners
about the complexity of life in the post-colonial state. The case of China in Sudan is
illustrative in this regard.

power dominance and control over the regime in Khartoum, but as Bayart (2000)

nature of the political economy of oil where huge investments in the form of sunk

towards the regime controlling the territory of oil (in this case Khartoum). Thus, when
the case of Darfur emerged on the international scene, it was not so much Beijing could
do than trying to persuade (mostly in vain) Khartoum and President Bashir to behave a
little better and be more responsible towards the population in this part of Sudan. This is
not an argument made to deny that a relationship of dependency does not exist between
African countries and important providers of capital and revenue, but that relationships
of dependency are never completely straightforward and there will always be ‘strategies
for the weak’ available, turning dependency from immobility to a mode of action. More
nuanced readings of the larger development studies literature could help illuminating

5.
Conclusion: the continued relevance of modernisation and dependency
theory
Development theory has been transformed through political shifts, changes in intellec-
tual currents and practical development experiences. These changes have often been
presented as paradigm shifts, and the representatives of new currents or ‘paradigms’
have often attempted to simplify their predecessor’s approaches in the processes of
highlighting their own contributions. In the process, also much important insight has
been lost. Currently, we therefore ﬁnd ourselves in a situation in which major contra-
dictions that have been treated in depth through the history of development theory
have resurged with full force, creating deep conﬂicts and contradictions that seem
very difﬁcult to solve.
The brief stories of the current experiences in Latin America and Africa illustrate
several points. First, it is obvious that in spite of its theoretical refutation and critique,
modernisation theory live on not only in recent theoretical work, but also in the devel-
opment strategies and discourses of current policymakers. While it was never comple-
tely off the agenda, for a couple of decades, the neo-liberal discourse with its focus on
markets as the main driving force of development was the dominating one. The ideas of
different contributors to modernisation theory with their focus on state-induced devel-
opment, technological progress and investments as a driving force of industrialisation
were seemingly ‘sent’ to the same resting place as ﬂat-earth manifestos and astrological
charts. However, with the success of China’s aggressive state-led industrialisation
catering to global markets, and the collapse of self-regulatory ﬁnancial markets in
the West, the ideas of modernisation have experienced an unexpected renaissance as
a model for development. However, when pursuing such strategies, countries have
come to ignore the long line of criticism launched against the modernisation project
as well as some of the more nuanced contributions to it. For example, some of the
Latin American regimes that are investing heavily in modernisation and that have
revived the role of the state in development ignore simultaneously the modernisation
schools’ recommendation of focusing on industrialisation, the warnings of the depen-
dency theorists against the establishment of capitalist ties to stronger powers and the
environmentalists’ insistence on sustainability as they are depleting natural resources
at a breath-taking pace. Moreover, while paying lip-service to post-colonial discourses
and new conceptions of the relationship between man and nature, they have only in
some cases taken seriously the task of mediating between different development

taught policymakers and their advisers of the perils of putting modernisation before
everything without taking into consideration the social, political as well as environ-
mental costs.
However, it is obvious that also dependency theory is still alive, and this is our
second main point. Among policymakers, it is perhaps more common to refer to depen-
dency mechanisms in discourse than in practice, but we can still observe efforts to
create alternatives to the existing western dominating capitalist system, as well as
‘real life’ evidence that the western dominance is waning. However, less attention is
paid to the new forms of dependency relations emerging in a world which is changing
much faster than what the old dependency theorists predicted, but which also shows
signs of conﬁrming some of their main hypotheses. The aggressive Chinese industrial-
isation strategy and its thirst for commodities has reduced the drive for industrial
upgrading in Latin America and created new dependency relations with Africa. This
relationship has so far not translated into a similar political power relationship as
seen with previous major economic powers, prominently the United States. Thus,
also the critiques of dependency theory that pointed to its excessive structuralism
and the relative lack of attention to the ideologies and strategies of individual actors
have regained relevance.
The greatest paradox of all is of course the main industrialised nations’ continued
reliance on mass consumption as the main driving force for development; in typical
Rostow style, they are ignoring the decades of warning of its limitations as a means
to improve the lives of the world’s underprivileged in a sustainable manner. There
are of course mainly political reasons for this glaring discrepancy between available
knowledge and actual policies, but many development researchers should also take
their part of the blame. In their eagerness to provide clear-cut categories as pedagogical
devices, they have often overlooked the continuities between different schools and
perspectives, and seeking to publish their own perspectives and results, they have
often contributed to shelving the ideas of past generations. We have argued here that
the ideas of the modernisation school as well as dependency theory are as vividly
alive as ever in development discourse, practice and as possible normative goals and
explanatory mechanisms in development. Thus, rather than being treated as odd
political prophecies, they should be continuously reﬁned, criticised and brought into
dialogue with competing viewpoints.
